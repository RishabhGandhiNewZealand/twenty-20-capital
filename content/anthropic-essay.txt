The $2 Trillion Hedge

When Anthropic eventually walks into the public markets, the number everyone will talk about is the valuation. If the rumors are right, that number could be close to $2 trillion, placing the company among the largest market debuts ever. It will invite the obvious comparisons, the predictable news segments, and the inevitable charts showing how few companies in history have ever been worth that much. Investors will ask whether a business still consuming extraordinary amounts of capital can possibly justify this valuation.

That is the obvious question. It is also only half the equation.

The conventional investment case for Anthropic is straightforward enough. Claude continues taking share in enterprise AI, coding becomes an enormous business, inference costs fall, margins expand, and Anthropic eventually grows into its valuation. Under that framework, $2 trillion looks demanding. Even if Anthropic becomes one of the most successful technology companies in history, investors buying at the IPO could still earn mediocre returns because so much of that success would already be embedded in the price.

Valuation therefore still matters. If Anthropic is a hedge, the valuation is the price of the hedge. Paying $2 trillion, $5 trillion, or $20 trillion for the same contingent payoff is not economically equivalent. The mistake is not to ignore valuation. It is to assume that Anthropic should be valued only by what its standalone cash flows look like in an ordinary future.

There is another dimension. Anthropic may have unusual value precisely in a state of the world that is unusually damaging to other assets. That state begins with an outcome speculative enough to resist conventional forecasting, but consequential enough that assigning it zero weight is equally unreasonable: Anthropic develops superintelligence first, and being first matters.

If that happens, the debate over whether Anthropic should trade at five or fifty times revenue becomes almost comically small. The company would no longer be selling enterprise software in any conventional sense. It would be selling intelligence itself, and intelligence is an input into almost everything else the economy does.

That possibility turns Anthropic from a normal investment into something much stranger. It is not insurance against transformative AI in general, because Anthropic can lose even if transformative AI arrives. It is a contingent hedge in the narrow world where cognition becomes radically more abundant, incumbent economics are impaired, and Anthropic captures a disproportionate share of the new rents.

Looking into science fiction we can frame this problem in a unique and holistic manner. In Isaac Asimov's Foundation, psychohistory works because the behavior of immense populations can be modeled statistically. Then the Mule appears, a singular actor whose existence falls outside the assumptions of the model and changes the path of history. The point is not that Anthropic is the Mule. It is that superintelligence may be a Mule-like event for financial markets. Investors are accustomed to debating probabilities inside a distribution. The critical possibility is that the event changes the distribution itself: which businesses are scarce, how labor and capital divide the surplus, and whether historical relationships remain useful at all.

What Happens If Intelligence Becomes Cheap?
Imagine Claude becomes capable of performing 70 percent of today's knowledge work. Not assisting humans with 70 percent of their work, but actually performing it. Software engineering, financial analysis, legal research, scientific discovery, accounting, marketing, product management, administration, design, consulting, and eventually large parts of corporate management itself.

The first instinct is to assume that Anthropic would behave like a traditional monopolist to capture extraordinary value. If Claude can replace a $200,000 employee, perhaps Anthropic charges $180,000 and pockets almost all the surplus.

It does not need to. More importantly, it may be strategically better not to.

The most useful analogy is TSMC. TSMC occupies one of the most strategically important positions in the global economy. For the most advanced semiconductors, many of its customers have few realistic alternatives. Yet TSMC does not attempt to capture all the economic value created downstream. Nvidia earns extraordinary margins selling chips manufactured by TSMC. Apple earns extraordinary margins selling devices built around processors manufactured by TSMC. Applied Materials, ASML, AMD, Broadcom, Qualcomm, and countless others have built immensely valuable businesses around the same manufacturing ecosystem.

That is not a weakness in TSMC's model. It is part of what makes the model so powerful. The ecosystem works because every participant has an incentive for it to grow. Anthropic could and should pursue the same strategy with intelligence.

The analogy proves something important, but narrow. TSMC shows how a foundational supplier can leave enormous value with customers and partners while itself becoming extraordinarily valuable. It does not prove that Anthropic can defend the same position. TSMC's barriers are physical, capital intensive, and painfully accumulated. Anthropic still has to earn its equivalent.

The economic structure required for the thesis is therefore more precise than simply saying intelligence becomes abundant. Intelligence must become abundant downstream while the production of the best intelligence remains scarce upstream. Claude could become cheap relative to human labor while access to frontier capability remains concentrated among a very small number of suppliers. That combination, cheap enough to spread everywhere and scarce enough at the frontier to preserve rents, is what creates the extraordinary economics.

If Claude can produce $200,000 of annual economic output, Anthropic could charge $20,000 and still create a staggering business. The customer receives most of the economic surplus. Anthropic captures only a fraction. But when that fraction is applied across tens or hundreds of millions of worker-equivalents, the numbers become difficult to conceptualize.

Even that framing may be too conservative because it treats the amount of intelligence demanded by the economy as roughly fixed. William Stanley Jevons noticed in the nineteenth century that greater efficiency in the use of coal did not necessarily reduce coal consumption. By making coal more economical, efficiency created new uses and increased total demand. Intelligence could behave in the same way.

If a unit of high-quality cognition becomes one hundred times cheaper, companies may not simply purchase today's quantity of thinking at one hundredth of the cost. They may purchase vastly more thinking: millions of simulations before making a decision, thousands of drug hypotheses instead of dozens, continuous redesign of products, permanent legal review, bespoke education for every student, and software written for tasks too small to justify a human engineer today. Most of the eventual demand may be for forms of cognition that are not currently purchased at all because human attention is too expensive, too slow, too scarce, and too inconsistent.

Jevons is not a law guaranteeing that total spending on intelligence rises as its unit price falls. That requires demand to be sufficiently elastic. The point is narrower: it is a mistake to assume that cheaper cognition necessarily produces a proportionately smaller revenue pool. If intelligence becomes useful in enough new places, lower prices could expand the market faster than they compress the price of each unit.

The ultimate addressable market for machine intelligence may therefore be much larger than the wage bill of the knowledge workers it replaces. It may include the enormous quantity of cognition humanity does not consume today because it has never been economically available. Superintelligence adds another layer because we have no reliable concept of the demand for cognitive capabilities that do not yet exist.

The company would not need to extract monopoly pricing to produce monopoly-like economics. It could be extraordinarily generous to customers and still generate levels of cash flow that dwarf today's largest businesses. This is also why Anthropic's public-benefit structure does not necessarily destroy the investment thesis. Anthropic could decide that broadly distributing the benefits of advanced AI is central to its mission, price Claude far below the theoretical maximum, and still become one of the most economically powerful companies ever created.

In fact, leaving money on the table may be exactly what maximizes the long-run value of the ecosystem. Amazon became more important because millions of companies could make money on AWS. Visa became more important because merchants could make money accepting Visa. TSMC became more important because Nvidia and Apple could become enormously valuable using its fabs. The most consequential infrastructure businesses do not need to consume the economics of the layers above them. They make those layers possible.

Anthropic's most extreme bull case is that it becomes the infrastructure layer for cognition while allowing enormous fortunes to be made both above and below it.

The Six-Month Question
Of course, none of this matters if model leadership remains temporary.

Claude can be the best model for six months. Gemini can lead for the next six. OpenAI can regain the lead after that. Chinese laboratories can close the gap. Open systems can commoditize the lower end of the market. In that world, Anthropic may still become a spectacular company, but it does not become the company described above.

The entire thesis depends on one question: can a six-month lead become unassailable?

I think it can. More importantly, that is how technological advantage often works once an industry becomes difficult enough. TSMC did not become TSMC because one process node was permanently superior. Google did not become Google because one version of search was permanently better. Meta did not become Meta because one social product won once. In each case, an initial advantage accumulated capital, data, process knowledge, distribution, customers, talent, and organizational capability that made the next advantage easier to earn. The lead became an input into producing the next lead.

There is no obvious reason AI should be exempt from that pattern. If anything, AI may be the most extreme version of it because the product can eventually participate directly in improving the process that creates the next product. The relevant question is not whether competitors can copy a particular model generation. They probably can. The relevant question is whether they can copy the rate at which the leading lab is learning.

Today, frontier AI research is constrained by human attention. A laboratory can raise another billion dollars, but it cannot instantly manufacture another thousand exceptional researchers. Hiring takes years. Training and knowledge transfer take years. Coordination costs rise. The best researchers remain scarce, and the number of experiments a human team can understand and direct remains finite.

A sufficiently capable AI researcher changes that production function. AI research labor is potentially replicable capital. If one instance can perform meaningful frontier research, then ten thousand instances may be deployed in parallel, provided enough compute and coordination are available. Better inference efficiency can make those researchers cheaper. A stronger successor model can make every one of them more capable. The laboratory no longer owns merely one extraordinary researcher. It owns software that can instantiate a research organization.

Consider a deliberately simple example. Suppose a weak form of superintelligence can produce a meaningful ten-percent improvement in a model in thirty days. The improved model completes the next research cycle in twenty-seven days, the next in roughly twenty-four, and the next in twenty-two. At the same time, inference improvements allow ten times as many research agents to work in parallel. The arithmetic is illustrative, not a forecast. Reality will impose bottlenecks. But the underlying change is real: the interval between improvements can begin contracting while the quantity and quality of research labor expand at the same time.

That is qualitatively different from a human-led flywheel. Human organizations can compound knowledge, but they cannot copy their best researchers at negligible marginal organizational cost. Machine researchers can, at least in principle. Their coordination may be imperfect, and many research tasks will not parallelize cleanly, but the ceiling is set increasingly by compute and physical infrastructure rather than by the supply of human attention.

The system can also begin attacking its own constraints. If compute is scarce, it can improve inference efficiency and training algorithms. If memory bandwidth is limiting, it can redesign architectures and kernels. If chips are limiting, it can help design chips. If data centers are limiting, it can optimize networking, cooling, utilization, and eventually robotics and physical construction. Fabs still take years to build. Power plants still exist in the physical world. Some experiments require calendar time that cannot be compressed by adding agents. The point is not that intelligence abolishes physics. It is that each bottleneck becomes another research problem for the intelligence itself.

The obvious objection is that Anthropic would not be alone. OpenAI, Google, Meta, and every other frontier laboratory would be trying to run the same loop. Positive feedback is not enough. For a six-month lead to become a six-year lead, Anthropic's rate of improvement has to accelerate relative to its competitors, not merely in absolute terms.

That is the real discontinuity on which the thesis rests. There may be some capability threshold at which being slightly better at AI research produces disproportionately better research output, which then produces a still better researcher. If the returns to automated research are roughly linear, the leaders trade places and the industry remains competitive. If they become sharply nonlinear, the first lab across the threshold may begin competing not with the models its rivals have today, but with the models its own superior system has already helped create.

The remaining technological uncertainty is therefore narrower than it first appears. It is not simply whether AI can assist AI research. It already can. It is whether marginal increases in intelligence continue producing sufficiently large increases in research productivity, or whether diminishing returns and physical bottlenecks overwhelm the loop. Anthropic does not need infinite acceleration. It needs its effective research velocity to separate enough from the frontier that rivals become trapped one or more generations behind.

Imagine Anthropic's relative research velocity moving from 1.0 times its competitors, to 1.1, then 1.3, then 1.7, then 2.5. The race can effectively end long before anything resembling a mathematical singularity. A rival can continue improving rapidly and still fall further behind because the leader is using its superior system to shorten the interval between each successive advance.

This leads to the cleanest falsification condition in the entire argument. Superintelligence needs to be hard, but achievable. If it is effectively a given along the existing research path, then being first matters much less. Anthropic might arrive in January, Google in March, OpenAI in June, and another lab shortly after. The world changes, but the intelligence layer looks more like a competitive commodity than a durable monopoly. If superintelligence is impossible or many decades away, the tail never arrives at all.

The extraordinary Anthropic thesis lives in the middle. The problem must be difficult enough that accumulated research advantage matters, but tractable enough that one laboratory can cross the relevant threshold and use the resulting system to widen the gap.

That difficulty is not a weakness in the thesis. It is the source of the moat.

Economists have begun building formal models of automated AI research that distinguish ordinary acceleration from explosive dynamics. The crucial condition is not simply that AI helps with research. It is that feedback from better AI into the production of still better AI becomes strong enough to outrun diminishing returns and the same feedback available to competitors. That is the six-month question in mathematical form: does the frontier move faster, or does the leader move faster than the frontier?

Revenue and compute then amplify the technological loop. A better product attracts more customers. More customers generate more revenue. More revenue purchases more compute. More compute supports more experiments and more machine researchers. Customers embed Claude more deeply into their organizations, developers build around it, and switching becomes progressively more painful. Scale can also produce earlier profitability and the ability to self-fund research if venture or public capital becomes less available. None of these effects creates the initial research lead, but each can make the lead harder to dislodge once it exists.

That is the scenario in which the moat stops looking like a conventional software moat.

When Power Starts Producing Power
Hamilton Helmer's Seven Powers framework is useful precisely because it forces a distinction between a lead and a defensible lead. Anthropic today can make a credible case for Scale Economies, growing Switching Costs, and the beginnings of Process Power. Brand is increasingly valuable, although much of it still reflects actual product superiority rather than customer preference independent of performance. Classical Network Economies are less obvious. More Claude customers do not automatically make Claude better for every other customer in the way more users strengthen a payments network or marketplace.

The more useful distinction is between advantages that can help an initial lead persist and advantages that appear only after Anthropic is already winning. Better security, privileged access to scarce compute, superior research judgment, and shorter learning cycles could help preserve an early lead. Deep enterprise integration and switching costs would emerge as customers reorganize around Claude. Brand, ecosystem breadth, and bargaining power would compound after dominance rather than explain how dominance began.

This is also where Cornered Resource and Process Power need to remain separate.

Process Power is Anthropic's accumulated method for producing frontier intelligence. It is the tacit and organizational capability that turns researchers, compute, experiments, evaluations, safety systems, infrastructure, and thousands of small operating decisions into better models. It is difficult to copy because the knowledge is distributed through routines, tools, culture, judgment, and a history of learning that cannot be purchased in one transaction.

The Cornered Resource would be the leading machine researcher itself, once it becomes materially better at producing its successors than any available substitute. It would not be the entire Anthropic organization, and it would not merely be a static set of model weights. It would be exclusive control of an intelligence whose economic value comes from its ability to create the next, better intelligence before competitors can reproduce it.

Before recursive separation, Anthropic may possess emerging Process Power but no true Cornered Resource. At the threshold, that superior process could produce the leading automated AI researcher. After separation, privileged access to that researcher becomes the Cornered Resource, while the surrounding organization that repeatedly turns it into better successors remains Process Power.

The distinction matters because the two Powers can then reinforce each other without becoming the same thing. Process Power creates the Cornered Resource. The Cornered Resource then improves Anthropic's Process Power by redesigning the research process, the tooling, the experiments, and eventually the physical stack on which the system runs. Power starts producing Power.

Getting there first matters because the resource renews itself. If Anthropic's machine researcher is better today, it helps create an even better machine researcher tomorrow. Rivals are not chasing a fixed frontier. They are chasing a frontier whose owner is using the frontier itself to move it.

The resource is cornered only if competitors cannot obtain an economically equivalent substitute before it improves again. If the weights are stolen, the methods diffuse, another laboratory independently recreates the system, or automated research benefits every frontier lab at roughly the same rate, the resource is not cornered and the thesis breaks. But if exclusive access persists across successive generations, Anthropic would control something genuinely scarce: not superintelligence as a static artifact, but the best available producer of future superintelligence.

Stealing one generation of weights would then be like stealing a photograph of TSMC's latest chip and assuming you had stolen the fab. The durable asset is not the photograph. It is the capability that produces the next node.

The Hyperscaler Paradox
Anthropic also occupies a peculiar strategic position because many of the companies that might ordinarily threaten it have economic incentives to support it.

Amazon and Google want Anthropic consuming enormous amounts of cloud capacity. Nvidia benefits when frontier laboratories continue turning capital into compute at extraordinary scale. Microsoft has reasons to ensure that Azure remains relevant to whichever models enterprises prefer. These companies may compete with Anthropic in one layer of the stack while profiting from it in another.

That matters because Anthropic does not necessarily need to own the world's data centers. It may be more powerful to have the world's data centers compete for Anthropic's workloads. If machine intelligence becomes the most valuable workload in computing, every hyperscaler has an incentive to make its infrastructure attractive to the companies producing that intelligence. Anthropic can sit above that competition, source enormous quantities of compute, and avoid total dependence on a single provider.

But the bargaining power cuts both ways. Amazon, Google, and Microsoft have every reason to want Anthropic to succeed as a customer, and every reason not to let Anthropic become an unavoidable tollbooth above their infrastructure. The same hyperscalers that compete for Claude's workloads can fund rival laboratories, build their own models, bundle intelligence into existing products, make switching easier, and preserve the customer relationship for themselves.

In other words, they may want Anthropic, but they may also want several Anthropics.

The extreme bull case therefore requires Anthropic to become important enough that infrastructure providers compete for it without becoming so dependent on those providers that they can commoditize the model layer beneath its feet.

The hyperscalers sell picks and shovels. Anthropic sells what the picks and shovels are being used to manufacture. Unlike gold, there is no obvious ceiling on the demand for intelligence.

Why $2 Trillion Might Be the Wrong Number to Obsess Over
None of this makes $2 trillion cheap. Under most conventional outcomes, it is likely expensive.

Anthropic could remain one of the world's best AI companies without ever achieving durable technological separation. Models could remain competitive. Margins could be lower than software investors expect because inference remains capital intensive. Customers could become adept at routing workloads between models. Open systems could compress pricing. Governments could regulate the industry aggressively. Application companies, hyperscalers, chipmakers, or energy providers could capture a larger share of the value than model providers.

Anthropic could execute extraordinarily well and still disappoint an investor who bought at $2 trillion.

There is also a dangerous intellectual trap here, one known for centuries. The St. Petersburg paradox describes a gamble whose expected monetary payoff can become absurdly large because sufficiently extreme outcomes dominate the calculation. Taken literally, the logic can justify paying almost any finite price for an equity. That is exactly the mistake an Anthropic bull has to avoid. If the argument becomes "perhaps it will be worth $100 trillion, or $500 trillion, or more," then every valuation can be made to look cheap simply by moving enough value into an increasingly fantastical tail.

The purpose of the tail scenario cannot therefore be to make price irrelevant. A $2 trillion Anthropic and a $20 trillion Anthropic are not the same investment. The higher the price, the more ordinary capital is sacrificed to preserve exposure to the extraordinary state, and the more extreme that state must become before the position matters.

The St. Petersburg objection is useful because it forces the thesis away from infinite-upside arithmetic and back toward portfolio robustness. The argument is not that the tail is so large that any price works. It is that the tail is unusual because its payoff may arrive when some other assets are impaired.

This is where conventional probability language becomes misleading. Whether the chance is 1 percent, 5 percent, or 10 percent matters enormously. The problem is that there may be no intellectually honest way to know which of those numbers, if any, is justified. The relevant state is unprecedented, path dependent, and potentially discontinuous. We do not have a historical distribution from which to estimate the odds that recursive AI research works, that a persistent winner emerges, that Anthropic is that winner, or that public shareholders capture the resulting rents.

That does not mean uncertainty itself is evidence for buying Anthropic. Otherwise every imaginable world-changing story would demand a place in the portfolio. A tail deserves serious portfolio consideration only when there is a credible causal path from present conditions to the extreme outcome, when the consequences are material to assets already owned, when the proposed position has unusually direct exposure to that outcome, when the carrying cost is survivable, and when the exposure may become difficult to acquire after the relevant state becomes obvious.

Anthropic is interesting because the argument can be made on each of those dimensions. Recursive research provides the causal mechanism. The erosion of cognitive scarcity provides the portfolio consequence. Anthropic equity would provide unusually direct exposure to one possible winner. A limited position makes the carrying cost survivable. The possibility of discontinuous validation creates the risk that the same exposure becomes much more expensive once it is easiest to justify.

This is the Foundation problem again. The failure mode is not necessarily that the forecast assigns the wrong probability to the Mule. It is that the Mule reveals the limits of the forecasting machinery itself. Superintelligence may or may not be that kind of event. But if it is, insisting on a neat actuarial probability can create false precision at exactly the moment the underlying structure is changing fastest.

What can be reasoned about is the asymmetry. If Anthropic makes cognition radically cheaper, some of the economic scarcities supporting today's corporate valuations could weaken. Businesses whose returns depend heavily on expensive cognitive labor, or on capabilities that new entrants could suddenly replicate, may face real pressure.

The damage would not be universal. Cheap intelligence could make many incumbents more profitable. Software companies could produce more with fewer engineers. Pharmaceutical companies could accelerate discovery. Banks could automate analysis. Firms with distribution, proprietary data, regulatory advantages, capital, or trusted brands might become stronger rather than weaker. The thesis does not require the existing stock market to collapse. It requires only that a meaningful portion of today's corporate value depends on cognitive scarcity that advanced AI could erode.

That is also why Anthropic is not a hedge against transformative AI in general. There are many worlds in which the disruption arrives and Anthropic fails to pay. OpenAI could win. Google could win. A Chinese laboratory could win. Models could remain competitive. Open systems could commoditize intelligence. Governments could regulate away extraordinary private rents. Value could accrue primarily to chips, energy, applications, or consumers.

The narrower claim is still powerful: if transformative AI both impairs parts of the existing portfolio and Anthropic becomes one of the dominant economic beneficiaries, Anthropic could offer state-contingent payoff alignment. It may move with the rest of the technology market most of the time and still become unusually valuable in the particular state in which some of those other assets are under the most pressure. That is different from ordinary negative correlation, and it is the more relevant property here.

The $2 Trillion Contingent Hedge
This is the part of the thesis that sounds almost backwards. You might knowingly buy Anthropic at a valuation you believe is too high. The stock might underperform for years. Multiples might compress. Every traditional valuation model might tell you that you overpaid. That underperformance is the cost of carrying exposure to a state of the world that ordinary valuation struggles to describe.

Calling it insurance goes too far because genuine insurance pays when the insured event occurs. Anthropic only pays spectacularly if two things happen together: advanced AI disrupts some of the economic assumptions behind the existing portfolio, and Anthropic becomes one of the dominant beneficiaries of that disruption. It is better understood as a highly specific contingent hedge.

That distinction also changes the position-sizing rule. A precise 4 percent, 5 percent, or 6 percent allocation cannot honestly be derived from probabilities and terminal values that nobody can estimate with confidence. The more defensible rule is simpler: the position should be large enough to matter if the extreme state arrives and small enough that being completely wrong remains survivable.

The appropriate size should depend on what the rest of the portfolio looks like. An investor whose wealth is concentrated in businesses that depend heavily on scarce cognitive labor has more reason to carry the position than an investor already concentrated in semiconductors, energy infrastructure, land, or other assets likely to retain value under a different economic structure. Exposure should rise with vulnerability to the state being hedged and fall with the cost of carrying Anthropic if that state never arrives.

That is a robustness argument, not an actuarial one. There are two possible mistakes. The first is owning some Anthropic at an absurd valuation and discovering that AI remains competitive, progress slows, Anthropic loses, or public shareholders capture far less of the value than expected. The loss is bounded by the amount invested. The second is owning none of the company that becomes a dominant supplier of cognition in a world where the same transition is eroding some of the assumptions underpinning the rest of the portfolio. The consequences of that mistake could be larger, correlated, and much harder to repair after the fact.

Other assets can hedge broader versions of the same future. Hyperscalers, chipmakers, energy infrastructure, and scarce physical assets may all benefit from transformative AI under different industry structures. I already view Alphabet and Meta as part of that broader exposure. But that does not make Anthropic redundant. Those companies own extraordinary incumbent businesses that may benefit from AI, may be disrupted by it, or may capture only part of the intelligence layer's economics. Anthropic would be a cleaner claim on the possibility that the frontier laboratory itself becomes the critical layer.

Nor does the thesis require Anthropic to be the only serious contender. I can identify several plausible frontier groups: Anthropic, Google, OpenAI, Meta, and leading Chinese laboratories such as Moonshot AI and Z.ai. The point is not that Anthropic is guaranteed to win. The point is that among the assets a public-market investor could realistically own, an Anthropic IPO may offer the most direct economic exposure to the winner-take-most version of the frontier-lab outcome.

In my judgment, Anthropic is also the leading laboratory today in the areas that matter most to this thesis. That lead may be only several months, and it may disappear. But the entire argument is that a several-month advantage matters if it is the advantage that crosses the recursive-research threshold first.

Why Not Wait?
There is an obvious strategy between buying Anthropic at the IPO and owning none forever: wait for evidence. Let the public market absorb the offering, allow the multiple to compress, observe whether automated AI research actually begins widening capability gaps, and buy only when the thesis starts to prove itself.

In many investments, that would be the disciplined choice. Here, waiting has an unusual cost.

You do not need to believe markets are perfectly efficient, or that every piece of evidence is instantly priced correctly. You need only believe there is a non-zero probability that thesis-validating information arrives before your preferred entry price does. Perhaps Anthropic trades down materially after the IPO and the research thesis remains intact. That would be a better investment. But there is another branch in which Anthropic releases sufficiently convincing evidence of recursive research acceleration before that cheaper price appears, and the market recategorizes the company.

The choice is therefore not simply "$2 trillion now or $1 trillion later." It is "$2 trillion now versus some probability of buying cheaper later and some probability that $2 trillion is never available again."

This is where the Six-Month Question and the timing question become the same argument. If machine researchers begin compressing the interval between breakthroughs, the interval between meaningful evidence points may contract as well. Anthropic's research cycle could be accelerating at exactly the moment an outside investor is waiting for another quarter, another model release, or another annual report. AI research can move toward machine timescales while portfolio decisions remain trapped on human timescales.

The machines may learn faster than the market can become comfortable with what they are learning.

That does not mean putting the entire intended allocation into the IPO. The more rational strategy is to establish a starter position large enough that the investor cannot be completely locked out of the tail, while preserving capital to respond as either price or evidence improves.

If Anthropic falls substantially and the recursive-research thesis remains intact, buy more because the same contingent exposure has become cheaper. If Anthropic rises but compelling evidence of research separation appears, buying more can also make sense because the probability distribution has changed even though the price is higher. If Anthropic falls because Claude loses frontier status, automated research appears to benefit every laboratory roughly equally, or the model layer commoditizes, do not mistake a lower price for a better investment.

Price and information jointly determine position size.

That is why I would buy at the IPO rather than wait for proof. The claim is not that proof and repricing must arrive at exactly the same instant. The claim is that there is a meaningful hazard they arrive close enough together that waiting for comfort destroys much of the option value. A distribution-changing event may not send polite advance notice. The investor who waits for proof may discover that proof was the repricing event.

The Spacing Guild Outcome
Frank Herbert's CHOAM is an intuitive analogy for a company sitting near the center of civilization's commerce, but the Spacing Guild may be the better one. The Guild does not need to own every planet, factory, or merchant house in the Dune universe. It controls an indispensable capability through which the rest of the economy must pass: interstellar navigation and transport. Its power comes from occupying a narrow layer that nearly everyone else needs in order to do anything at scale.

Anthropic's extreme bull case has that shape. It would not own the global economy. It would own, or share ownership of, a critical cognitive layer through which an increasing amount of the global economy operates.

Anthropic would not need to own every application. It would not need to replace every corporation. It would not need to confiscate every dollar of economic value created by AI. It might be more powerful if it did none of those things.

The hyperscalers make money supplying compute. Developers make money creating applications. Entrepreneurs build companies with ten employees that previously required ten thousand. Pharmaceutical companies discover drugs faster. Manufacturers redesign products continuously. Banks automate analysis. Governments become more efficient. Scientists compress decades of research into years.

Everyone can profit.

Anthropic simply charges for the intelligence making it possible.

A small toll on an enormous amount of global economic activity can become a very large number. Visa does not need the merchant's revenue. TSMC does not need Nvidia's gross margin. AWS does not need to own its customers. Anthropic would not need to capture the world's entire economic surplus. It would need only to become the layer that the world increasingly cannot function without.

The Dune analogy also contains its own warning. The Spacing Guild's apparent indispensability rests on another scarce input: spice. Anthropic has its own version of spice in compute, semiconductors, electricity, and physical infrastructure. A company can dominate one layer of a system while remaining dependent on a bottleneck somewhere else. The fact that Amazon, Google, Microsoft, Nvidia, and other infrastructure providers have incentives to support Anthropic is therefore both a strength and a reminder that intelligence is never produced in a vacuum.

There is an even stranger science-fiction limit to the bull case. Iain M. Banks imagined the Culture as a civilization of extraordinary machine intelligence and material abundance in which traditional money and scarcity have largely lost their organizing role. If superintelligence ultimately pushes the economy anywhere near that direction, the idea of valuing Anthropic at $50 trillion or $100 trillion may itself become conceptually unstable.

The investable opportunity may therefore be concentrated in the transition rather than the final equilibrium: the period when machine cognition has become extraordinarily productive and scarce at the frontier, while conventional property rights, prices, and corporate claims still determine who captures the surplus. Anthropic does not need to rule a post-scarcity civilization forever for the equity to matter. It needs to capture extraordinary rents during the passage from human-scarce cognition to machine-abundant cognition.

Success taken to its absolute limit could eventually consume the unit in which the success is being measured.

The Verdict
That is why the $2 trillion number, enormous as it is, may not be the only number that matters in the Anthropic IPO. The more difficult variable is one we may not be able to express honestly as a number at all.

Transformative AI is not a roulette wheel with known probabilities. It could change which forms of scarcity have economic value, how labor and capital divide the surplus, which companies possess durable moats, and whether historical correlations remain useful. Demanding a precise probability before allowing any exposure can create the illusion of rigor while quietly assuming that the future is more measurable than it is.

The stronger question is one of regret under deep uncertainty. How painful is it to own some Anthropic and be wrong, compared with owning none and being wrong in the one state where Anthropic becomes a foundational supplier of cognition?

That does not make valuation irrelevant. The price determines how much exposure you can afford and how much capital you sacrifice in the ordinary worlds where the thesis fails. It does mean that a conventional discounted cash flow may understate the portfolio value of owning an asset whose maximum payoff could arrive alongside impairment elsewhere.

The investor considering Anthropic is therefore not simply choosing between a $2 trillion stock and a cheaper software company. The investor is choosing how much exposure to carry to a particular possible future, one that may be too uncertain to model cleanly but too consequential to treat as impossible.

At a roughly $2 trillion IPO valuation, I would buy Anthropic. The only question is how much.

That distinction matters. The thesis determines participation. Price and evidence determine position size. At a higher valuation, I would begin with less. At a lower valuation, I would own more. If the price falls while the thesis remains intact, I would add. If the price rises because credible evidence of recursive research separation appears, I may also add. If the technological premise breaks, valuation alone would not rescue the position.

In most futures, paying $2 trillion for Anthropic may look aggressive. In one future, it could look almost absurdly cheap.

The most persuasive argument against buying Anthropic at $2 trillion is that the evidence is not there yet. That is also the argument for buying it.

If the evidence were already there, Anthropic would not be offered at a valuation designed for a merely exceptional technology company. It would be valued as something else entirely. The investment therefore depends on an uncomfortable proposition: you have to own the asset while the extraordinary thesis is still unproven because the moment it is proved may also be the moment the market stops offering you the same bet.

If Anthropic never crosses that threshold, $2 trillion may look ridiculous. If superintelligence turns out to be easy and every frontier laboratory arrives within the same window, the thesis breaks. If the problem is hard but achievable, and Anthropic gets there first with a machine researcher capable of making itself better faster than rivals can follow, then the category changes.

And $2 trillion may be remembered not as the price at which investors lost their minds, but as the last price before the market understood what kind of asset it was looking at.
