# The $2 Trillion Hedge

*Why we would still buy Anthropic at a $2 trillion IPO valuation*

---

When Anthropic walks onto the public markets later this year, the number everyone will talk about is the valuation. If the rumours prove accurate, the offering could value Anthropic near $2 trillion.[^1] Many investors will ask whether a company still consuming extraordinary amounts of capital can possibly be worth that much. That is the obvious question. It is far from the only one.

The conventional case is easy enough to understand. Claude keeps taking enterprise share. Coding becomes a huge business. Inference costs fall, margins improve, and Anthropic grows into the valuation. Under that framework, $2 trillion is demanding. Anthropic could become one of the most successful technology companies ever built and still deliver mediocre returns to someone who buys the IPO.

Anthropic possesses unique characteristics that could make it immensely valuable in a future that is destructive to other portfolio assets. That future is speculative, but plausible enough that we cannot dismiss it as impossible: Anthropic develops superintelligence first, and getting there first matters greatly.

If that happens, debating whether the company deserves a valuation of five or fifty times revenue becomes beside the point. Anthropic would be selling raw intelligence, not software: an input into nearly everything else in the economy.

Anthropic could still lose in an era of transformative AI. The thesis is narrower: the position hedges a decline in the cost of cognition that severely impairs incumbent economics within a portfolio, provided Anthropic captures a large share of the available profits. This classifies Anthropic as a state-contingent hedge rather than a normal investment.

Isaac Asimov's *Foundation and Empire* offers a useful way to frame this problem. Psychohistory works because the behaviour of immense populations can be modelled statistically. Then the Mule appears: a singular actor outside the model's assumptions who changes the path of history. Anthropic is not the Mule, although superintelligence might be.

Investors like to assign probabilities within a distribution. Superintelligence could be an event that changes the distribution itself: which businesses are scarce, how labour and capital divide the surplus, and whether historical relationships remain useful at all. In that world, Anthropic should be viewed less as a normal equity investment than as protection against an extreme tail event.

## Consequences of cheap intelligence

Imagine Claude can perform 70 percent of today's knowledge work. The first instinct is to imagine that Anthropic prices like a monopolist. If Claude can completely replace a $200,000 employee, perhaps Anthropic charges $100,000 and pockets the difference. However, it does not need to.

TSMC is a useful comparison. It occupies arguably one of the most important positions in the global economy, yet it leaves enormous value for the companies around it. Nvidia and Apple earn extraordinary margins using chips manufactured by TSMC. That is not a failure of value capture; it is a deliberate choice that fosters a virtuous ecosystem. This ecosystem works because everyone has a reason to make it bigger.

Anthropic will pursue something similar with intelligence. If Claude produces $200,000 of annual output, charging only $10,000 may be sufficient. The customer keeps most of the surplus. Anthropic takes only a fraction. Applied across tens or hundreds of millions—perhaps even billions—of worker equivalents, that fraction still creates a staggering business.

The analogy is slightly flawed. TSMC's barriers are physical, capital-intensive and painfully accumulated over decades. Anthropic must earn something comparable first. For the thesis to work, intelligence must become abundant downstream while the production of frontier intelligence remains scarce upstream.

Demand is also likely to be much larger than the current knowledge-worker wage bill. Cheaper cognition does not necessarily mean buying the same amount of thinking for less money. Companies will buy far more of it: thousands of drug hypotheses instead of dozens, constant product redesign, permanent legal review, millions of simulations before a decision, software for jobs too small to justify a human engineer—the list goes on.

This is the Jevons effect applied to intelligence. Efficiency gains made coal useful in more places and increased total consumption. Cognition will behave in the same way, intelligence demand has to be elastic. Today, cognition is limited by human attention and is expensive, slow and scarce. As we find it hard to believe today's quantity of purchased thinking is anywhere near a natural limit or that such a limit even exists. 

Anthropic would not need to extract every possible dollar. It should price well below the theoretical maximum, ideally an order of magnitude below, remain consistent with a public-benefit mission, and still become one of the most powerful businesses in the world. Infrastructure companies often become more important by allowing fortunes to grow around them. AWS did. Visa did. TSMC did.

There is a tendency to make the addressable market calculation too tidy. Start with the global knowledge-worker wage bill, assume a percentage gets automated, give the model provider some share of the savings, and out comes a very large spreadsheet number. We doubt the economy will cooperate with that model. Some jobs will disappear. Others will change. Entire categories of cognitive work that no company buys today may become normal operating expenses. The number of “workers replaced” may eventually be less useful than the volume of machine cognition consumed. We do not measure cloud computing by counting the server-room employees AWS replaced.

The extreme case is this: machine intelligence makes cognition cheap, fast and abundant. Demand for intelligence is unbounded. Anthropic becomes the infrastructure for cognition and lets others make plenty of profit above and below it.

## The six-month question

None of this matters if model leadership remains temporary. Claude can lead for a few weeks, Gemini for the next three, then OpenAI. Chinese labs can close the gap, and open models commoditize the lower end. Anthropic might still be a very good company in that world, but it would never become the infrastructure described here.

The question is whether a six-month lead can become extremely difficult to catch.

There is precedent, although this remains the least certain and most important part of the thesis. TSMC did not win because one process node stayed permanently superior, nor did Google win because one version of search was permanently better. An early lead attracted capital, talent, customers and process knowledge, making the next lead easier to earn. AI could become the strongest version of this pattern because the product may eventually help create its successor. Competitors can copy features and perhaps entire model generations. The harder thing to copy is the rate at which the leading lab learns.

Frontier research is still constrained by human attention. A lab can raise another ten billion dollars. It cannot instantly create and integrate another thousand exceptional researchers. A capable machine researcher changes that production function. Suppose an early superintelligent system can improve a model by 10 percent in thirty days. Its successor completes the next cycle in twenty-seven days, then twenty-four, while efficiency gains allow more research agents to work in parallel. The numbers are illustrative. What matters is that research cycles could shorten while research capacity expands.

This simplifies the problem, and physics still gets a vote. Fabs and power plants take years to build; some experiments cannot be parallelized; and ten thousand research agents will not be ten thousand times as productive as one. Better intelligence may ease these constraints by improving algorithms, chip design, networking and cooling, but it cannot make the physical world disappear. Importantly, these constraints apply to every frontier lab; the thesis requires Anthropic to improve faster than its rivals.

Imagine two labs advancing at roughly the same rate until one develops a slightly better AI researcher. If that advantage produces only a proportionate increase in research output, little changes. The labs continue trading places and the model layer remains competitive. But if the better researcher is disproportionately effective at building its successor, the race changes. One lab begins shortening its research cycles while the other is still trying to match its previous generation.

This does not require infinite acceleration or a cinematic singularity. Anthropic only needs enough of a lead to remain one or two generations ahead. Once that separation appears, ordinary business advantages can reinforce it. The better model attracts more customers; revenue funds more compute and experiments; developers build around Claude; and switching becomes harder. These forces cannot create the initial breakthrough, but they can turn it into a durable advantage.

There are many ways the loop could stall. Research agents might repeat one another's mistakes or produce mountains of plausible junk that humans must inspect. Capability gains could diminish, and physical bottlenecks could dominate. “Recursive self-improvement” can hide more than it explains. The loop does not need to be magical. It only needs to become strong enough at one lab to make the next round unforgivably unfair.

This leaves the thesis in a narrow middle. If every frontier lab arrives within a few months, being first creates little moat. If the technology is impossible or decades away, the tail never arrives. Anthropic's extraordinary outcome exists between those extremes: it crosses the sufficiently hard threshold first, then turns a temporary lead into a widening one.

## When Power starts producing Power

If Anthropic crosses that threshold, a temporary lead could begin turning into something more durable. Hamilton Helmer's Seven Powers framework helps separate that lead from an actual moat.

Today, Anthropic can already make a case for Scale Economies, emerging Switching Costs and some Process Power. Brand matters, although much of the Anthropic brand still reflects current product quality. Traditional Network Economies are less obvious. Anthropic does not yet possess a Cornered Resource, nor does it have a clear Counter-Positioning advantage against frontier competitors.

For Anthropic, the clearest route from these partial advantages to a moat is for one Power to begin producing another. That is when a self-reinforcing lead could become defensible. The natural path is to use its emerging Process Power to create a Cornered Resource. That resource could then strengthen the Process Power that produced it, while reinforcing Scale Economies and Switching Costs around both.

### The self-renewing resource

Process Power is Anthropic's accumulated method for producing frontier intelligence: its researchers, infrastructure, evaluations, safety systems, tooling, judgment and thousands of operating decisions. It is difficult to copy because much of that knowledge lives in routines and people rather than in a manual. That process could eventually produce the best automated AI researcher. The leading machine researcher could then become a Cornered Resource. The Cornered Resource would not be a static set of model weights. It would be Anthropic's ability to put that intelligence to work inside a research system that produces the next generation faster than anyone else. The moat would live in the whole machine. Control of that machine would help Anthropic improve the process that produced it. This is why getting there first matters: the resource renews itself. Rivals would not be chasing a fixed frontier. They would be chasing a frontier whose owner is using it to move the frontier.

The resource is only truly cornered, however, if competitors cannot reproduce an economically equivalent capability before Anthropic advances to the next generation. If the underlying production capability diffuses or another laboratory independently recreates an economically equivalent system, the resource is not cornered and the thesis breaks. But if exclusive access persists across successive generations, Anthropic would control something genuinely scarce: not superintelligence as a static artifact, but the best available producer of future superintelligence.

In that world, stealing one generation of weights alone would be like stealing a photograph of TSMC's latest chip and assuming you had stolen the fab. The durable asset is not the photograph. It is the capability that produces the next node.

### The early Anthropic signal

Our contention is that, among the companies competing at the frontier, Anthropic may be closest to setting this process in motion. Across the four months since Opus 4.7's release, Anthropic briefly lost the lead, took it back, and then held the frontier on Artificial Analysis's composite Intelligence Index. Opus 4.7 launched in a tie for first; Opus 4.8 retook the lead in late May; and Opus 5 and Fable 5 currently remain first and second in Artificial Analysis's index despite releases from all other labs.[^2] The fact that Anthropic has stacked leading model releases in a market where model-level leads often last only weeks is the earliest flicker of Process Power, albeit not proof of it [^3]. A lead of a few months is not yet a moat. But if progress becomes harder, the same head start could carry Anthropic across the threshold first. Beyond that point, the lead will begin to widen rather than close.

Its train may be about to leave the station while others are still preparing to board.

## The Spacing Guild constraint

If Anthropic's Process Power produces a true Cornered Resource, the two could reinforce each other in the way described above. But control of the best machine researcher would not give Anthropic control of every scarce input that researcher needs. Its cognitive moat would still rest on a physical stack owned largely by other companies.

Amazon and Google want Anthropic's cloud spending; Nvidia wants frontier labs turning capital into compute; and Microsoft wants Azure to remain relevant regardless of which model enterprises prefer. This dependence gives Anthropic options. Rather than owning the world's data centres, it may be better to have infrastructure providers competing for its workloads.

We can already see this diversification: Anthropic has committed to spend more than $100 billion on AWS technologies over ten years and $30 billion on Azure compute, while *The Information* reports a further $200 billion, five-year commitment to Google Cloud and chips.[^4] The hyperscalers are competing to generate Claude tokens. The bargaining power cuts both ways. Amazon, Google and Microsoft have no reason to welcome an unavoidable tollbooth above their infrastructure. They are funding rival laboratories, building their own models, bundling intelligence into existing products and making switching easier. They want Anthropic. They also want several Anthropics.

Frank Herbert's Spacing Guild is a useful analogy. The Guild does not own every planet, factory or merchant house in *Dune*. It controls interstellar navigation, the indispensable layer through which the rest of the economy must pass. Anthropic's extreme bull case has the same shape. It would not need to own every application or capture every dollar created by AI. A small toll on the world's cognitive activity could become an enormous business. But the Guild's power still depends on spice. Anthropic's spice is compute, semiconductors and electricity.

The best machine researcher could make that dependence less constraining, turning Anthropic into an even stronger version of the Spacing Guild—one capable of improving the equivalent of interstellar travel itself. In practice, that means better training algorithms, lower inference costs, better chips and more intelligence per unit of power. Anthropic would still need the physical stack, but it might use that stack more productively than its competitors. The bull case therefore requires Anthropic to become important enough that infrastructure providers compete for it, while its research advantage reduces its dependence on them faster than they can commoditize the model layer. Anthropic would remain dependent on the infrastructure without remaining hostage to it.

This is how a six-month lead becomes durable: Process Power creates the machine researcher, the machine researcher improves both the process and its physical inputs, and each turn of the flywheel pushes the frontier further out of reach while progressively insulating Anthropic from constraints it cannot control.

## Why $2 trillion might be the wrong number to obsess over

So far, we have described the most extreme version of the Anthropic bull case. But even if much of that future arrives, Anthropic could still disappoint an investor who buys at $2 trillion.

Models may remain competitive, inference may stay capital-intensive, customers may route workloads between providers, and open models may compress prices. Much of the value could accrue to chips, energy, cloud platforms, applications or consumers rather than to the model providers themselves.

Even technological success may not translate into shareholder returns. Anthropic could accept lower returns to satisfy its safety and public-benefit mission. Governments could mandate broad access, cap prices or treat frontier intelligence as strategic infrastructure. Compute providers may negotiate away much of the surplus. A spectacular outcome for the technology is not automatically a spectacular outcome for the equity.

There is also a dangerous trick buried in the bull case. The St. Petersburg paradox shows how a tiny probability of an enormous payoff can dominate an expected-value calculation. Applied carelessly, that logic can justify buying any equity at any price: perhaps Anthropic will be worth $100 trillion, $500 trillion or more. It is easy to unconsciously smuggle price-indifference into the thesis by inventing a sufficiently large tail. A $2 trillion Anthropic and a $20 trillion Anthropic are not the same investment. The higher the price, the more capital we must sacrifice today and the more extreme the winning future must become.

The difficulty is that we cannot assign an honest probability to the extraordinary outcome. Is it 1 percent, 5 percent or 10 percent? We have no idea, and neither does anybody else. There is no historical distribution for recursive AI research, persistent lab dominance, an Anthropic victory and public shareholders retaining the rents—only anecdotes. Returning back to the world of *Foundation*. The Mule does not merely reveal that psychohistory assigned the wrong probability; it exposes the limits of the forecasting system itself. Superintelligence may not be that kind of event. But if it is, demanding a quantitative estimate creates false precision at exactly the wrong moment.

However, uncertainty alone is not a reason to invest. Otherwise, every company with a promise of a better future deserves a place in the portfolio. What makes Anthropic interesting is the combination of a credible mechanism, a material consequence for assets already owned, direct exposure to a possible beneficiary and the risk that this exposure becomes far more expensive once the thesis is obvious.

## Anthropic's purpose in the portfolio

We cannot predict this future, but we can reason about its asymmetry. If Anthropic makes cognition radically cheaper, some of the scarcities supporting today's corporate valuations could weaken. Businesses whose returns depend on expensive cognitive labour, accumulated know-how or capabilities that new entrants cannot easily reproduce may come under pressure once those advantages can be replicated at low cost.

That does not require mass bankruptcy or a market collapse. Capital can be destroyed through lower barriers to entry, weaker margins and reduced terminal values. A business may become more productive while its competitive advantage becomes less valuable. The benefit of cheaper labour can accrue to customers and new competitors rather than to the incumbent's shareholders.

The damage would not be universal. Cheap intelligence could make many existing companies more profitable. Firms with distribution, proprietary data, regulatory advantages, capital or trusted brands may become stronger. The thesis only requires a meaningful part of today's corporate value to depend on cognitive scarcity that advanced AI could erode.

Anthropic is therefore not an ordinary negatively correlated asset. Its appeal is that it could become unusually valuable in the specific future where it wins the intelligence layer while that same transition weakens parts of the existing portfolio.

### Enough to matter

The narrow possibility that Anthropic pays off precisely when advanced AI is destroying value elsewhere in a portfolio gives it an option-like role and shifts the practical question from whether to own it to how much.

We might buy Anthropic at a valuation we consider too high and then watch it underperform for years. That underperformance is the cost of maintaining exposure to a future that conventional valuation methods struggle to capture.

We can simplify the situation into two possible errors. One is buying Anthropic at an absurd valuation and watching AI progress slow, the industry remain competitive or Anthropic lose. That loss is bounded by the amount invested. The other is owning none of the company that becomes a dominant supplier of cognition while the same transition damages assumptions supporting the rest of the portfolio. That error could be correlated across several holdings and difficult to repair after the fact. The second error is far costlier than a small underperformance penalty.

The right balance depends on the rest of the portfolio. An investor concentrated in businesses built on expensive cognitive labour has reason to own more Anthropic than someone already exposed to semiconductors, energy infrastructure or other likely beneficiaries of AI. There is no honest way to derive a precise 4, 5 or 6 percent allocation from probabilities we cannot estimate. **The thesis determines participation; existing exposure, valuation and emerging evidence determine size.**

Our portfolio already has mild exposure to likely beneficiaries through the hyperscalers, semiconductor companies and other infrastructure plays. That reduces the need for a large Anthropic position, but it does not make direct exposure redundant. A tail allocation of roughly two percent therefore seems appropriate: large enough to matter if the thesis is right, but small enough that a complete loss would not materially impair the portfolio. This is a risk-budget judgment, not a probability-derived optimum. At $2 trillion, our judgment is that a two-percent position retains enough upside in the tail to help offset impairment elsewhere while a total loss remains tolerable. If the valuation made the payoff from a tolerable position too small to matter in the winning state, we would pass.

## Why not wait?

A two-percent allocation answers how much to own. The remaining question is when.

The disciplined strategy seems obvious: let the IPO settle, wait for the multiple to compress and buy once we have clear evidence that recursive self-improvement is underway. Usually, this would be prudent. However, the risk with Anthropic is that the evidence arrives before an attractive entry price ever does.

After the IPO, Anthropic may trade down 70–80 percent while the research thesis remains intact. In that world, it becomes a much more attractive investment. But recursive research acceleration could instead become clear soon after the IPO and cause the market to recategorize the company. The choice is therefore not simply $2 trillion now or perhaps $500 billion later. It is $2 trillion now versus some chance of a cheaper entry and some chance that the same exposure is never again available near $2 trillion.

There is another reason not to wait. If machine researchers begin to compress the interval between breakthroughs, the interval between investable evidence points may also compress. Research could move on machine timescales while investors wait for quarterly reports on human timescales. That argues for establishing a starter position at the chosen allocation, then letting price and evidence jointly determine future actions. Add if the price falls and the thesis remains intact. Add if credible research separation raises the odds, even at a higher price. But if Claude falls materially behind the frontier and fails to regain it, or the model layer commoditizes, a lower price does not improve the investment. At that point, we will likely sell.

## Verdict

> **At an IPO valuation of roughly $2 trillion, we would buy Anthropic and allocate two percent of the portfolio.**

Not because $2 trillion looks conventionally cheap. In most futures, it will not. We are buying exposure to a narrower outcome: machine cognition becomes radically cheaper, weakens the scarcities supporting parts of the existing portfolio, and Anthropic becomes a foundational supplier of that cognition. Because its largest payoff may arrive while other holdings are being impaired, owning none could prove more damaging than carrying a small, expensive position.

The thesis rests on a specific technological condition. Superintelligence must be hard but achievable; Anthropic must cross the threshold first; and its machine researcher must improve faster than rivals can follow. That would turn a temporary lead into a self-reinforcing combination of Process Power and a Cornered Resource. Public shareholders must then retain a meaningful share of the resulting rents.

We cannot assign an honest probability to that future. Transformative AI may change the distribution itself—which capabilities remain scarce, which companies retain their moats and how labour and capital divide the surplus. False precision would not improve the decision.

That is why we size for regret rather than expected value. Two percent is enough to matter if the tail arrives, but small enough that underperformance or a complete loss does not materially impair the portfolio. At a higher valuation, we would own less; at a lower one, more. Credible evidence of recursive separation could justify adding even at a higher price. If Anthropic falls materially behind the frontier and fails to recover, if the model layer commoditizes or if the recursive-research premise breaks, a lower valuation will not rescue the thesis, and we will exit.

**The evidence is not there yet.** That limits the position, but it is also why we would establish it now: proof may become the repricing event.

In most futures, $2 trillion will look aggressive. In the one we are hedging, it may look cheap.

## Further reading

These works develop the essay's fictional frames, strategic vocabulary and central economic and technological mechanisms. They are background reading rather than evidence for the Anthropic-specific investment claims.

- **The Mule and psychohistory:** Isaac Asimov, [*Foundation and Empire*](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/5657/foundation-and-empire-by-isaac-asimov/).
- **The Spacing Guild and spice:** Frank Herbert, [*Dune*](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781427201447/dune/).
- **Process Power, Cornered Resource and the broader strategy framework:** Hamilton Helmer, [*7 Powers: The Foundations of Business Strategy*](https://7powers.com/buy-the-book/).
- **Efficiency, falling costs and expanding consumption:** William Stanley Jevons, [*The Coal Question*](https://www.econlib.org/library/YPDBooks/Jevons/jvnCQ.html), the original source behind what later became known as the Jevons paradox.
- **Tiny probabilities and enormous payoffs:** Martin Peterson, [“The St. Petersburg Paradox”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/paradox-stpetersburg/), *The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*.
- **The original intelligence-explosion argument:** I. J. Good, [“Speculations Concerning the First Ultraintelligent Machine”](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0065245808604180), *Advances in Computers* 6 (1965), 31–88.
- **A contemporary Anthropic view of recursive self-improvement:** The Anthropic Institute, [“When AI builds itself”](https://www.anthropic.com/institute/recursive-self-improvement).
